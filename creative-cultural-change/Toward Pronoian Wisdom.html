Toward Pronoian Wisdom

Wisdom at large involves deep imagination.

Craig Chalquist - Chalquist.com 

December 2, 2024; updated September 1, 2026

 

There is a kind of refusal to serve power that isn’t a revolt or a rebellion, but a revolution in the sense of reversing meanings, of changing how things are understood. Anyone who has been able to break from the grip of a controlling, crippling belief or bigotry or enforced ignorance knows the sense of coming out into the light and air, of release, being set free to fly, to transcend.

–Ursula K. Le Guin, Afterword from Tehanu

Those who dream by day are cognizant of many things which escape those who dream only by night.

The great problems are to be encountered in the street.
 –Nietzsche, Daybreak


 Wisdom is not just for elite thinkers atop mountain peaks or secluded in august halls of learning. To remain relevant and vital, wisdom must get dirty, go outside, and bring us together. And its origins must be in an often-rejected alchemical stone: imagination.

To understand why, let us consider a crisis involving wisdom itself. It is fictional, except it isn’t. We will also look at a few definitions of wisdom and see where imagination comes in.

The Castalia Crisis

A certain passage in Hesse’s last novel The Glass Bead Game always pains me to read. It is Joseph Knecht’s final circular letter to the Board of Educators of Castalia, a future institute of researchers, contemplatives, and philosophers. Cloistered from the hurly-burly of ordinary life, Castalians have dedicated themselves to preserving the fruits of Mind in a great synthesis they call the Glass Bead Game. Only the brightest lights can gain admittance to Castalia, and only the brightest of the bright can play the Game.

One of these is Joseph Knecht, who after many years of training and self-cultivation becomes Castalia's leader.

One day, Knecht—whose name means "knight" and connotes service—begins to think about how Castalia has cut itself off from the world around it. Castalians are too busy intellectualizing to dirty their hands with politics, finance, economics, social justice, or even history, which to them means Jesus and Bach, Pascal and Plato, not the revolutions, bloodbaths, and upheavals no detached Castalian could be bothered to consider.

Nor did Castalia have any aims beyond "egotistic enjoyment of its own overbred faculties." What did the crass outside world have to do with the arts, thought, meditation, reflection, deep inquiry, or self development? In this aloofness Knecht recognized the vulnerability and ultimate transience of a beloved institution.

The educators, he realized, needed educating.

So he wrote down his worries, beginning with: "I have begun to doubt my ability to officiate satisfactorily because I consider the Glass Bead Game itself to be in a state of crisis.” He then compares Castalia to a school whose roof is on fire and himself to a resident scholar calling out a warning.

The average Castalian may regard the man of the outside world, the man who is not a scholar, without contempt, envy or malice, but he does not regard him as a brother, does not see him as his employer, does not in the least feel that he shares responsibility for what is going on outside in the world.

It is not that the specialized studies and reflective practices shouldn't be done or aren't valuable. They are, even essential. It's that how they are done distracts from concerns rising on every side. Also, they are performed with a certain arrogance.

....This Castalian culture of ours, sublime and aristocratic though it certainly is, and to which I am profoundly grateful, is for most of those associated with it not an instrument they play on like a great organ, not active and directed toward goals, not consciously serving something greater or profounder than itself. Rather, it tends somewhat toward smugness and self-praise, toward the cultivation and elaboration of intellectual specialism.

Citing the Castalian teachers sent into the world as the only exceptions to this, Knecht continues:

Above all, we forget that we ourselves are a part of history, that we are the product of growth and are condemned to perish if we lose the capacity for further growth and change. We are ourselves history and share the responsibility for world history and our position in it. But we gravely lack awareness of this responsibility...     

Knecht points out that although Castalia arose in part as a reaction to days in which things of the Mind served only political or propagandistic ends, and although the institute rightly safeguards higher learnings against such mass sellouts of the past, Castalia has come to represent the other extreme: philosophy, truth, spirit, and culture so detached from the ordinary aspects of life that Castalia has become "ripe for dismantling" by historical forces it does not deign to examine.

He goes on:

But although we have almost forgotten it, "Magister Ludi" of course originally meant not the office we have in mind when we use the word, but simply schoolmaster....Teachers are more essential than anything else...That is where the basis for the cultural life of the country is to be found, not in the seminars or in the Glass Bead Game.

Then he resigns, leaves Castalia, and becomes a teacher to the outside world.

For more than two decades, and mostly in higher ed, I have had the privilege of offering deep education, a term I coined to describe transformational whole-person learning rooted in depth psychology, archetypal theory, ecopsychology, terrapsychology, dream studies, mythology, qualitative research, consciousness studies, inner work, somatics, the study of symbols, Family Systems psychotherapy, and alchemy as a wisdom path. I’ve designed and taught more than fifty graduate courses, launched certificates and specializations, mentored doctoral students through dissertation, chaired departments, co-led academic conferences.

At the same time, I’ve kept one foot planted in the soil outside higher ed: workshops, study groups, public programs, public lectures and discussions, civic and business presentations, corporate consulting, retreat center offerings, continuing ed, outreach to activists, volunteering, podcasts, panels, and interviews. I’ve spoken to organizations as far apart on the political spectrum as Harvard Radcliffe and Modern Day Rifleman. Neither of my PhDs included any instruction on how to bring what I had learned into the world. I had to figure it out as I went.

As I’ve done all this, publishing books and chapters along the way, I’ve watched the schools and programs I worked for succumb to bureaucracy, political infighting, overregulation, and self-constriction. Higher ed’s knee-jerk response to being attacked by anti-intellectual bigots is often to contract into self-defeating defensiveness: more rubrics, more evidence, more stringency, all to convince people who won’t ever be convinced of the value of what we do as we go on attacking one another.

I have even had to fight for my faculty to use books more than five years old. Can you imagine? There are courses we can’t teach—and wisdom we can’t offer—because certain books were never published as e-texts. In one of them, the great Sufi philosopher Ibn 'Arabi is quoted as saying that hardened doctrines are a form of idolatry.

Many of these transformative programs have fallen into the same trap that damaged the Human Potential Movement: overfocus on individual enlightenment and underfocus on the collective concerns of the day. As though journaling and meditation could heal the world all by themselves! This smacks of an omnipotent fantasy mixed with denial and a dollop of narcissism.

And so programs that could be radically transmutative—and are at the individual level—die off one by one as depth psychology is excluded from “evidence-based” curricula, departments of philosophy further refine abstractions, and the US Congress kills student loan programs and cuts funding. 

Where are we to go for wisdoms to live by?

What Is Wisdom?

Wisdom is easier to recognize than to define. We can all think of people we consider wise, but what makes them so? They seem to have an extra dimension to them, a depth of knowledge beyond the technical or academic. We might define wisdom as insightful, holistic, and deep knowing: true understanding in contrast to avidya, or ignorance, which is different from simply not having been taught something. Avidya is more like clinging to biases we won’t let go of, or persisting in delusions, or refusing to be mentally flexible enough to change our minds.

Robert McDermott’s definition links wisdom to ethics: “Wisdom is deep or uncommon knowledge essential for living and a foundation for right action” (Philo Sophia). It requires no wisdom, only technical and marketing know-how, to build an atomic bomb or let loose AI on an unsuspecting public. Wisdom asks, “Why do this? For whose benefit? What might be the consequences?”

Wisdom for Aristotle is a fuller kind of knowing that includes prudent practicality (phronesis). In the Bhagavad Gita, wisdom is a precious space of refuge to be sought. In the Katha Upanishad, wisdom is a seeing through to the forms and forces below appearances. In the Quran, wisdom is a seed in the heart that needs nourishing, as when wise humans align with the essential nature of humanity (al fitra). The word “sage” shares etymology roots with the “sap” of living trees. In the Book of Proverbs, wisdom (Chochmah) is the “master builder” of everything.

The world’s folklore contains many wisdom figures, usually depicted as goddesses and wise feminine beings. A mythic definition of wisdom would be: to cultivate a conscious, devoted relationship with Sophia. “You don’t believe in Wisdom,” says Rabbi Rami Shapiro, “you engage with her.” She has many archetypal sisters: Ma’at, Hellenistic Isis, Pronoia, Athena, White Buffalo Calf Woman, Star Woman, Saraswati, Amaterasu, Fatima, Nuwa, Nzambi…

Jung spoke of a Wise Old Man archetype, but the imaginal wisdom-giving figures he writes about in his journals identified themselves as wizards. In Islam, God’s essence (al-Daht) is a feminine term. In myth, male gods who bring wisdom are usually taught it by goddesses.

Wisdom is related to depth: seeing below surfaces and appearances, as depth psychology does, to the images, dreamings, mythic motifs, and other organizing structures below actions, thoughts, and events. Wise people tend to be deep people.

The wisdom we normally picture should perhaps be called Castalian wisdom: cloisered, specialized, difficult to access, sometimes expensive, and owned mostly by specialists who deploy technical vocabularies. What else is available?

Pronoian Wisdom

Fortunately, there is plenty of wisdom beyond the ivory towers, and its teacher practitioners are numerous. A few names that come to mind are Kwame Scruggs, Amanda Leetch, Kathy Stanley, Matthew Cochran, Susan Grelock, Kimmy Johnson, Lali Mitchell, Michael Meade, Jeanine Canty, Betsy Perluss, Joanna Macy, Victoria Loorz, Sandy Hessler, Linda Buzzell, Boston Blake, Kelly Moore, Shmee Giarratana, Sandra del Castillo, Minerva Arias, Jun Wang, Catherine Svela, Melinda Coles, Ryan Hurd, Karen Jaenke, Charles Burack, Bruce Alderman, Britt Wray, Akila Rayapuraju, Jan Edl Stein, Garret Barnwell, Jurgen Kremer, Sigmar Schwarz, Lisa-Marie Persaud, Mathew Mather, Lyn Mather, Mark Boyden, Carmen Sorrenti, and many others. Some work in academia, but not only there.

Wisdom literally, mythically, and archetypally means “love of Sophia.” Pronoian wisdom, love of Sophia as Pronoia, is a state of open, imaginative receptivity to patterns, images, and symbols moving underneath the surfaces of events; thoughtful and honest self-inquiry on how this knowledge is received; and sincere reflection on possible humane, relational, and mature responses as a result.

Let’s look at each of these three movements:

1. Imaginative receptivity

The capacity for reading events symbolically for the movements behind, below, or within them goes by various names. In the psychological sphere, Jungian James Hillman refers to “psychologizing,” or seeing through, events and actions, symptoms and dreams to the underlying action of mythic motifs, as when we get stuck in the Hero psychology of trying to fix things by force of will, or when (to use Glen Slater’s example) the sinking of the Titanic repeats the Greek myth of the Titans’ downfall and imprisonment in the deepest abyss. (See his paper “Re-Sink the Titanic.”)

Muhyiddin ibn ‘Arabi’s move of ta’wil (symbolic interpretation) parallels seeing through but is not just psychological. Ibn ‘Arabi disagrees with Qur’anic Yusuf (Joseph), for example, that the sun, moon, and stars bowing to him in a dream stand only for his parents and brothers. He would also disagree that the celestial lights were mainly mythic or archetypal. For Jacob, who also interprets the dream, its ta’wil points further to Yusuf’s divine mission; in other words, to the realm of spirit. Ta’wil moves from lower to higher, from tangible to imaginal and beyond, and never in the other direction. 

Although neither seeing through nor ta’wil, the knowledge of historical patterns is a form of wisdom that can illuminate contemporary events and decisions. The repeated blunders of Western military incursions in West Asia (the Middle East) is one of many examples of action without wisdom. When the US President decided to attack Iran in 2026, one of the first protests came from his own generals. They knew the relevant history and could imagine the disastrous results. Another example is Project 2025, which would be hard to surpass as a shadowy list of the most oppressive and self-defeating policies imaginable. It might as well have been named Project Ruin USA.

Imagination is central to wisdom. No aspect of human consciousness lives apart from our imaginings. In Islamic gnosis, imagination opens the door to the alam al-mithal, the world of images, called “imaginal” (coined by Henry Corbin) to distinguish this realm “between” matter and spirit, sense and intellect, from what we think we just make up. For Ibn ‘Arabi, the Arabic word khayal means much more than fiction or fantasy. It is a realm of being. In many traditions, imagination is also a path of deep knowing (see my dissertation Restorying Our Lore).

2. Thoughtful and honest self-inquiry.

In Toward a Psychology of Being, Abraham Maslow attributes this to Freud: “To be completely honest with oneself is the very best effort a human being can make.” This is Maslow rather than Freud, but it’s certainly in the spirit of what Freud taught about the need for complete self-honesty even when difficult. He attempts this in The Interpretation of Dreams and made it a personal practice.

When psychotherapists talk about being able to “think psychologically,” they mean in part a capacity to wonder about one’s unconscious motives. Jungians talk about taking our shadow into account. Philosophers have long recommended honest self-inquiry—”Know Thyself”—even when we resist it, as in what Sartre calls bad faith (mauvaise foi): making excuses for when we act inauthentically or deceptively, for example. It’s so very easy to lie to ourselves.

I used to have a cartoon of a psychologist wrapped in strings of Christmas lights. The caption said that he felt relief sitting down to write his new book on how attraction to lighting is healthy and fun. I think also of thick tomes of philosophical systems and arguments sitting next to an empty spot on a desk where analyses of intellectual narcissism should stand. When we engage in a particular piece of psychologizing, imagining, or philosophizing, what are we really prompted by? Power drive, ambition, egotistical streak, control game, genuine desire to educate others? All of the above?

3. Reflection on responses.

Jorge Borges’s story “The Aleph” describes a point in space, a small portal, in which one can see anything in the entire universe. What is done with this marvel? A poet hides it in his basement to help him win poetry contests. A visitor allowed to see the Aleph eventually convinces himself that it is too marvelous to exist. Thus do we tarnish our most significant glimpses into the transcendent. (Here in the US, someone who got ahold of the Aleph might well charge an exorbitant subscription fee for an occasional glance into its depths.)

Having seen through and inquired into how what we see lands in us, how are we to respond?

A while back, I coined “imaginal liberty” to mean two things: the freedom to imagine without constraints, and respect for the autonomy of the imaginal. Try to drag imagination into immediate usefulness and it is liable to dry up. But if we don’t wonder at some point how what we have learned can address urgent current concerns, we are back in the Castalia problem, where our visions remain disconnected from life. Visions that count must at some point be implemented.

Let it be a rhythm, then. Start with free imagining and follow wherever it leads without saddling it with concerns about utility: in other words, to paraphrase Tolkien and Le Guin, without collapsing the world of imagination into the dayworld. At a point we can learn to be sensitive to, we may then ask how the imaginal gifts we received can benefit others—but not before that point.

We might also bear in mind that wisdom is not just an individual acquisition.

Mythologically, Pronoian wisdom invokes the goddess Promoia, who for the Greeks was the wife of foresightful Prometheus. She saw what was coming. Later, she appears as a Gnostic goddess similar to wise Sophia. In this form, Pronoia repeatedly descends into human life to nourish those who seek her counsel. What she brings comes before intellectualizing or abstraction: her name means "before mind." "Pronoia" psychologically is the opposite of paranoia. We might say "pronoiac" wisdom, but "pronoian" suggests membership in a community.

Wisdom At Large

The traditional Jungian side of the wisdom house (to use them as one example) has been particularly resistant to showing up for any community but its own. Fights about who belongs in Jung’s lineage and who doesn’t. Condemnation of advocacy or activism as inflated rescuing or acting out. Self-development as a panacea for cultural change. I once had to teach Gnosticism privately to analyst candidates because their institute wouldn’t allow it from anyone who wasn’t a Jungian analyst. More recently, I was told that discussing American politics during my online Jungian course was too controversial. None of this was personal: other depth-oriented teachers have similar obstacles to deal with.

Separating inner from outer, surrendering to unconscious forces, succumbing to elitism, getting stuck in archaic imagery to the exclusion of new possibilities, and keeping deep wisdom out of public view aren’t wise. They exhibit splitting, denial, a “narcissism of small differences” as Freud put it, shortsightedness, and sheer lack of realism. As a result, Jungian programs wither while the work of evolving rather than enshrining Jung’s thought goes on elsewhere: in bold Friends of Jung circles, in artists’ studios, in film, in literary clubs, in dance, in innovative therapies and MFA programs.

All these creatives are dancing with Pronoia. She is like her archetypal sisters Sophia, Athena, Nuwa, Saraswati, White Buffalo Calf Woman, Star Woman, Hellenistic Isis, Ma'at, Nzambi, and Sri Lalita, the Hindu goddess who preceded and created Triumurti, the three big gods who think they run the cosmos. Lalita's name means “She who plays.”

Pronoian wisdom dances with them. Maybe we should too.

Why do we need wisdom in the streets and outside the hallowed halls? So entire misled populations don’t elect dictators. So people can be empowered to demand limitations on industries that harm the planet and its creatures, including us. So we can get at the underlying factors and motivations for our worst choices and make changes instead of relying on easy explanations that go nowhere. (“Fathom” originally meant “embrace,” then “depth”: getting to the bottom of things.)

So we can educate wise leaders instead of malignant narcissists who care only about themselves. Unwise leadership is killing us.

So politics, religion, finance, industry, medicine, agriculture, education, technology, and other contemporary institutions and forms of organized power can serve us and the world instead of reducing us to the distracted parts of a machinery of disheartening soullessness.

So we can raise boys to respect women, women to spot and reject abuse, and everyone to respect the fluidity and diversity of gender and sex and ways to love.

So we can be free of the forces that oppress us from the outside in and the inside out.

To accomplish all this, wisdom, equipped with imagination, must go outside.

Yes, inner work is necessary. So are all our specialized Castalian depth studies. Now, however, given the state of the rapidly declining world, we must apply those learnings where they can do the most good. The hermit hiding in a cave is not the resource we need today, and the rainmaker whose self-adjustment ended a drought lived in a younger and smaller world. That kind of wisdom, though valuable, isn't enough now.

When fully effective, Pronoian wisdom fosters mutually transformative encounters between wisdom and public concerns in service to enlightenment, community, justice, healing, and humaneness. Inner and outer are not split. The goal is to become fuller and more mature human beings in our dealings with ourselves, each other, and our troubled homeworld. This wisdom is visionary, grounded, metanoic, creative, and expansively Jupiterian rather than rigidly Saturnian in flavor. Wisdom obeys no rubric. 

I never liked that Hesse's tale ended with the death of Knecht. I understand the noble sacrifice, but I would rather he had gone forth to found his own new learning center better engaged with the times.
